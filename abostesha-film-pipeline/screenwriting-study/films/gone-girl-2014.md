# Gone Girl (2014) — screenplay study

## 0. Draft facts
- **Writer (verified, PDF p. 2):** Screenplay by Gillian Flynn, based on her novel. The credit is repeated on p. 3.
- **Draft as printed:** Twentieth Century Fox, FINAL SHOOTING SCRIPT, August 29, 2013, revised September 15 and 27, 2013 (p. 2). Colour log (p. 3): White 7/30, Blue 8/29, Pink 9/15, Yellow 9/27/13. Headers mark 111 Blue, 53 Yellow and 9 Pink pages.
- **Length:** 177 PDF pages: cover, title page and revision log, then script pp. 1–163 (PDF pp. 4–177), including 11 A-pages.
- **Scenes:** numbered in both margins: A1, 1–273 and 16 letter inserts. 28 are marked OMIT, leaving about 262 live scenes.

## 1. Format & page organization
- **Numbering and revisions.** Added scenes get letters (A61/B61; A178–C178) and added pages get A-numbers. OMIT slugs are kept (28, including a run of ten on pp. 126–127). Asterisks in the right margin mark revisions, and every header names its colour. There are no CONTINUED headers; (MORE) appears 9 times and CONT'D about 170 times.
- **Sluglines.** The standard INT./EXT. – PLACE – TIME. INT./EXT. is used for car shots (pp. 29, 58), and some ESTABLISHING slugs carry no action (pp. 30, 31, 84). Times go beyond DAY and NIGHT: DAWN, PRE-DAWN, DUSK, SUNSET.
- **The diary label is the key device.** Each entry is its own scene headed `INT. SOMEWHERE - SOMETIME` (p. 6), followed by a close-up of a different pen, a printed date and Amy's V.O. reading the words. There are ten such scenes (pp. 6–86). Her V.O. then carries on over ordinary sluglines, so the diary *owns* those flashbacks. The prologue shares this floating time (INT. BEDROOM – SOMETIME, p. 4).
- **Nick's clock.** 26 title cards give the date plus a counter, from the morning of July 5 (p. 4) to NINE DAYS GONE (p. 153). A card often lands after a scene's first line, like a button (pp. 69, 120, 127).
- **Amy's clock.** After the twist the count rewinds to 11:17 a.m. (p. 87) and then ticks hourly, always at :17:
  ```
  TITLE CARD:
  JULY, 5, 2012
  1:17 PM
  TWO HOURS GONE
  ```
  (p. 93). Her thread replays days one to three (pp. 100–102) and syncs with Nick's at FOUR DAYS GONE (p. 106). Later the count jumps to 30–40 days (pp. 154–157), and the unit flips from GONE to HOME (pp. 166–173).
- **Transitions.** There is no CUT TO. BLACK SCREEN marks the three hinges (pp. 4, 87, 158), FADE OUT/IN bridges the three-week jump (p. 154), and the script ends on FADE TO BLACK (p. 177).
- **Devices.**
  - INSERT appears four times (pp. 42, 48, 59, 146) and ON TV about 13 times; a TV is on about 20 pages.
  - There are no MONTAGE, INTERCUT, FLASHBACK or SERIES OF SHOTS headers. Montages are runs of one-line scenes (pp. 87–92), and crosscuts alternate full slugs (pp. 85–87, 134–138).
  - **V.O.:** Nick has V.O. only in the prologue (p. 4); his reprise is cued O.S. (p. 177). Amy has about 86 cues: 34 before p. 85, 49 on pp. 85–99, and 3 after. The clue poems are heard in Amy's V.O. inside Nick's scenes (pp. 41–48, 85).
  - O.S. appears about 15 times, and underlining marks spoken stress (pp. 17, 43, 92, 93).

## 3. Twist engineering
**The twist in one sentence:** Amy is alive. The missing wife whose diary narrates the first half wrote it as evidence: she staged her own murder and laid a treasure hunt of clues to put her cheating husband on death row.

**Misdirection toolkit**
- **POV control.** Amy's present is hidden for 81 script pages. Nick's secrets (the burner, his dread at dawn) are hidden too, so we share the cops' suspicion.
- **Genre expectation.** The cops recite the true-crime template aloud (pp. 21–22), and Ellen Abbott is the chorus.
- **Emotional distraction.** The fairytale entries buy the voice our trust (pp. 10, 30–31), and Amy later names this as her strategy.
- **Red herrings.** The exes; the drug-ridden mall (pp. 65–68); Noelle's claim to be Amy's best friend; Nick's change of clothes (p. 5).
- **Withholding.** Nick's divorce plan (p. 98) and the "Mr. Brown" story behind Clue 2 (p. 122).
- **Editing grammar.** Amy's voice reads the clues over Nick's actions, as if she were accusing him live.

**The diary as deliberate unreliable narration**
- *It performs sincerity.* Dates, distinct pens, pink handwriting, self-mockery, and hedges in which she doubts herself (pp. 74, 86). An accuser who doubts herself sounds credible.
- *It has an authored arc.* Nine entries, 4–14 pages apart, run from 2005 to the morning of July 5, 2012, with the key ones on anniversaries and Valentine's Day. They move from romance to strain to fear to pregnancy to the fear that he will kill her.
- *The images comply.* The push (p. 65), the cold sex (p. 63) and Nick watching her (pp. 73–74) are filmed with no flag that they are subjective.
- *The world corroborates it.* Each claim has a witness or a document. Only Nick's counter-account (pp. 82–83) argues back, and it is less charming.

**The reveal (pp. 85–97, about 13 pages in three stages)**
1. **The turn (pp. 85–87).** The diary's date reaches the title-card date. The camera finally rises to Amy's face, stork pen in hand (p. 85), as she watches Nick's dawn from p. 4. One V.O. sentence runs over four crosscut scenes: Nick scribbling, Boney at the furnace, Nick running, the diary closing. Boney pulls out the diary, the woodshed door opens on Nick, and then:
   ```
   BLACK SCREEN
   AMY (V.O.)
   I'm so much happier now that I'm dead.
   ```
   (p. 87). Fade in: Amy speeds away and snaps the pink pen from entry one.
2. **The how (pp. 87–92).** The card rewinds to that morning. A to-do list opens a second-person montage that re-stages earlier clues from her side. Its keystone is *"Start with the fairytale early days—those are true and those are crucial. You want Nick and Amy to be likable. After that, you invent: The spending, the abuse, the fear, the pregnancy, the murder."* (p. 92)
3. **The why (pp. 93–97).** The Cool Girl V.O. (section 4), ending as she hammers her own cheek.

The staging uses a prop, a voice, a time card and a recap montage; no two characters share the reveal. **The audience re-reads** every entry, every clue, the day counter and the prologue.

**Fair-play verdict.** Largely fair. The honest clues:
- the SOMEWHERE – SOMETIME label;
- Boney calls the scene staged, and Nick asks why a stager would mop (pp. 78–79);
- the upright frames;
- a clue left where the police would find it (p. 36);
- a heroine literally rewritten as fiction (p. 17).

The one cheat is the push (p. 65), a lie filmed as an objective image. It is licensed by the convention that V.O. owns its images, and Amy retracts it on the page (p. 92).

## 4. Dialogue craft
- **Voice.**
  - *Nick* deflects with ill-timed pop-culture jokes (pp. 23, 49) and shrinks to fragments under questioning (pp. 24–26).
  - *Amy* speaks in lists (p. 9), anaphora and verdicts: sugared on camera, an essayist in V.O., a commander after the twist.
  - *Go* is profane sibling shorthand, with X-is-code-for-Y glosses (pp. 33, 70).
  - *Boney* sets traps disguised as box-ticking (p. 25); *Tanner* talks in showbiz aphorisms; *Ellen* asks tabloid rhetorical questions; *Desi* speaks in formal possessives.
- **Subtext.**
  1. Nick's TV plea (p. 146) is an apology to the nation and a code to Amy.
  2. Desi's tour of his luxury house (pp. 140–141) promises safety and builds a cage.
  3. Amy editing the adjectives in Nick's interview answers (pp. 172–173) is editing the man.
- **Exposition hiding.**
  - A game of LIFE carries the anniversary history (pp. 11–12).
  - A tour of book posters delivers Amy's childhood (pp. 16–17).
  - An interrogation played like a quiz shows Nick knows nothing about his wife (pp. 24–26).
  - TV pundits deliver the evidence (pp. 71–72, 113–117).
  - Boney's true-or-false reading of the diary dramatizes the half-true method (pp. 151–152).
- **Rhythm.** Exchanges run 1–3 lines, with dashes for cut-offs and shared sentences (p. 22). Long speeches are saved for set pieces. Silence is written as action (pp. 37, 57). For counterpoint, the Gettysburg Address runs under an interrogation (pp. 66–68), and Amy heckles Andie's press conference (pp. 136–137).
- **Lines that change meaning after the twist.**
  - Nick calls the treasure hunt a forced march (p. 11).
  - Amazing Amy is always one step ahead of the real Amy (p. 19).
  - Clue 2's couplet casts the couple in a husband-and-wife role-play (p. 42).
  - Andie doubts Nick would kill for her (p. 137), answered later by Amy's claim to have killed for him (p. 175).
- **Excerpts.**
  - *"When I think of my wife, I always think of her head."* (p. 4). A tender image with violent subtext; the hook returns at the end with a new weight.
  - *"Cool Girl is fun. Cool Girl is game. Cool Girl is hot. … Cool Girl never gets angry at her man."* (p. 94). **This is the model of reveal-by-voice.** 16 V.O. blocks over pp. 93–96 move from thesis to definition, then to culture (three passing girl types illustrate it), then to confession, grievance and verdict. Meanwhile the images take the persona off: her hair is cut and dyed, she eats junk food, and she trades size-2 jeans for a size-8 dress. Hourly cards keep the clock moving, and the speech ends in an act that becomes evidence (p. 97).
  - *"I have taken myself to the woodshed over the way I treated you. Come home and I will spend the rest of my life making it up to you."* (p. 146). Double address: contrition for the public and a clue-word for Amy.
  - *"You begged for me to save your life on national TV. And I obliged. But I want that Nick."* (p. 165). She married the performance and wants it back.

## 5. Directing actors on the page
- **Parentheticals.** About 99 in 163 pages (about 0.6 per page): addressee (~20), timing (~14), physical business (~35) and tone (~25). A tone cue is usually one loaded word (dark, pointed, dissolving). The best one names the word the actor must swallow: *"(don't say "amazing"...)"* (p. 45).
- **Behaviour carries emotion.** Nick squeaks a Styrofoam cup until Boney's hand stops him (p. 25), switches an oily smile on and off (p. 39) and hits the dashboard three times (p. 48).
- **Pauses carry adjectives:** painful, ugly, sick (pp. 18, 119, 175).
- **Colon subtext.** A look is written as an unspoken line: *"She gives a look: Talk."* (p. 6). There are about a dozen more (pp. 33–173), and one kiss gets two opposite subtexts at once (p. 68).
- **Performance within performance.** Action lines name the persona Amy is playing (survivor, p. 158), and characters rehearse in mirrors (pp. 90, 172).

## 6. Writing images, VFX & sound
- **One image plus one simile.** *"SLOWLY, the area begins glowing like a neon Jackson Pollock: Wild sprays of blood all over the wall."* (p. 75). Action blocks run 1–4 lines in the present tense, with capitals on key nouns.
- **The impossible image.** Amy's imagined corpse gets an ordinary slug, INT. WATER – DAY, not FANTASY. A V.O. sentence begun in the car carries over into it: *"Splash. Amy's body cuts through the water feet-first. She's bound but already the binds are unraveling. One arm comes loose and it trails along lazily behind her as if she's waving goodbye."* (p. 92). The image escalates to drowned women and fish in her hair (p. 93).
- **Implied camera.** "We see" and "we hear" appear about 31 times; CLOSEUP and POV are rare. Late in the script the camera becomes part of the story: security feeds show the staged captivity (p. 155). Violence is implied through a mirror (p. 156) or written in kinetic verbs that end on a smile (p. 157).
- **Sound.**
  - The alarm's REEK (p. 47).
  - The Gettysburg Address (pp. 66–68).
  - The word pregnant echoing across the park (p. 77).
  - A jackpot ringing as Amy hooks Desi (p. 134).
  - The robot dog barking through Nick's ruin (p. 149).
  - Toilet flushes as punctuation (pp. 115, 172).
  - A pre-lap across the fade (p. 154).
  - A snapped pen hitting a road sign like a gunshot (p. 87).
- **Transitions as storytelling.** Nothing is labelled MATCH CUT; instead the prose names each rhyme. Amy sees Nick as we first saw him (p. 86); snow recalls the sugar (p. 104); the book-party greeting is reused (p. 134); the first shot is repeated (pp. 166, 177).

## 7. Theme, motifs, meaning
- **Central dramatic questions.** What happened to Amy (answered p. 85)? Can Nick out-narrate her (pp. 146–158)? Can either of them leave the marriage? The answer is no (pp. 175–177).
- **Theme: marriage as a co-authored performance.** Identity is a role written for an audience: for parents (Amazing Amy), for lovers (Cool Girl; the better man Nick promised to be, p. 146) and for the nation (TV). The theme is stated four ways: as questions (p. 4), as Clue 2's role-play (p. 42), as manifesto (pp. 93–96) and as verdict (p. 175).
- **The hidden theme is authorship.** Nick's BOOK IDEAS file is empty (p. 42), Amy out-writes him (p. 92), and Boney and Nick review the diary like critics (pp. 120, 152).
- **Media as plot engine.** Every turn is a public event: the press conference (p. 39), the selfie on TV (p. 71), the vigil (p. 77), Andie's press conference (p. 137), the Schieber interview (p. 146), the doorstep return (p. 158) and the final Ellen interview (pp. 173–177). Amy's plan needs public hatred (p. 92), Nick's TV win triggers her return, and public opinion locks him in (p. 165).
- **Motifs.**
  - the back of Amy's head (pp. 4, 177);
  - the pens;
  - clues and board games: Master Mind, Clue, LIFE (pp. 5–13);
  - sugar and snow, with the lip-brush (pp. 10, 104);
  - the chin code (pp. 10–171);
  - trash and disposability (pp. 4, 29, 83, 166);
  - sheet thread counts as a measure of class (pp. 32, 95, 141);
  - puppets (pp. 62, 98);
  - the cops crossing off leads against Amy's checkbox list (pp. 25, 87–89);
  - the lentil (pp. 85, 175);
  - the glow of TV lights (p. 166);
  - a counter that turns from GONE to HOME.
- **Final image (p. 177).** It mirrors p. 4 and adds one question about the future. Her alarmed look becomes a haunting smile: she has won, and the performance is now permanent.

## 8. Lessons for a 7-minute twist/VFX short
1. **An honest but opaque label** — The diary is headed SOMEWHERE – SOMETIME (p. 6), and the payoff shows where it was written (p. 91). — Head every "dream" materialization with one neutral slug that stays true under both readings. Never write DREAM.
2. **Two clocks that collide** — The diary dates meet the GONE counter (p. 85), and the card rewinds (p. 87). — Show the man's session counter on screen; at the twist, one SUPER gives the wife's real count (days since the accident).
3. **Tag every "invention" with a prop** — Each entry has a new pen, and all of them end up on her car seat (p. 87). — Give each appearance of the woman one object (ring, scar, song); at the twist, those objects sit in the wife's bag.
4. **Split the reveal sentence, then go to black** — See pp. 86–87. — One V.O. sentence over three images, then black, then one line of under 10 words. The turn takes 20–30 seconds.
5. **Re-stage the opening from the other side** — See pp. 4, 86 and 177. — Show her materializing from his POV; at the twist, show the same moment from her side, at the console; end on the opening frame with one detail flipped.
6. **Plot first, then character by voice** — The how-montage (pp. 87–92) comes first; then Cool Girl is spoken while she physically un-masks, ending in an act (pp. 93–97). — After the twist, give the wife 30–45 seconds of voice over a concrete task (removing electrodes, packing the "dream" props).
7. **A gesture as proof, then as power** — The chin code (pp. 10, 146, 171). — Plant a gesture he thinks he invented; at the twist it proves she is real; at the end he makes it unprompted.
8. **A physical test with two payoffs** — The frames are noticed, tested and explained (pp. 14, 78, 91). — He tests the dream (a fingerprint on glass); the audience reads a VFX glitch; the twist shows plain physics.
9. **Let the narrator own the images, then retract one** — The push (p. 65) is later confessed as a lie (p. 92). — After the twist, replay one dream shot without his V.O.; it reads differently.
10. **Look-lines and button cards** — See pp. 6, 69 and 120. — Write looks as unspoken lines, and drop SUPERs after a beat so they land as revelations.

## 9. البطاقة العربية
- **الفيلم · الكاتب · عدد الصفحات:** «Gone Girl» (2014) · سيناريو جيليان فلين عن روايتها (صفحة العنوان، ص 2) · 177 صفحة PDF. النسخة المطبوعة: «نص التصوير النهائي» المؤرخ 29 أغسطس 2013، مع مراجعات في 15 و27 سبتمبر 2013.
- **التويست في جملة:** إيمي لم تُقتل، بل اختفت عمدًا. هي من كتبت اليوميات التي سمعناها، وزرعت أدلة "جريمة قتلها" لتدفع بزوجها الخائن نحو حكم الإعدام.
- **آلية التويست:**
  - ساعتان متوازيتان: عدّاد «أيام الغياب» في خيط نيك، وتواريخ اليوميات في خيط إيمي. يلتقيان عند 5 يوليو 2012 في منتصف النص تمامًا (ص 85–87)، ثم يعود العدّاد إلى صباح يوم الاختفاء ليبدأ من جديد من وجهة نظرها.
  - راوية غير موثوقة مصمَّمة بإتقان: لكل مدخل قلمه الخاص وتاريخه، وتسبق كل مدخل ترويسة INT. SOMEWHERE – SOMETIME صادقة لكنها غامضة. الصور تجسّد أكاذيب اليوميات دون أي إشارة إلى أنها ذاتية، ثم تعترف إيمي بالطريقة كاملة (ص 92).
  - شبكة تصديق: لكل كذبة في اليوميات شاهد أو وثيقة في العالم الخارجي (تاجر المخدرات، الجارة، السجل الطبي، الديون، وكوخ الحطب).
- **سرّ الحوار:**
  - لكل شخصية بصمة لغوية تُعرَف بها دون اسمها: سخرية نيك المراوِغة، وقوائم إيمي وأحكامها القاطعة، ومصطلحات غو الفظّة.
  - الكشف بالصوت: مونولوج «الفتاة الرائعة» يكشف الشخصية بعد أن كُشفت الحبكة، بينما تخلع إيمي قناعها جسديًا أمامنا. مثال: "Cool Girl never gets angry at her man." (ص 94).
  - خطاب بوجهين: اعتذار نيك المتلفز موجّه إلى الجمهور، لكنه في الوقت نفسه رسالة مشفّرة إلى إيمي (ص 146).
- **إدارة الممثل على الورق:** الإرشادات بين القوسين قليلة ومحددة، وتحمل السلوكيات المشاعر: كوب الستايروفوم، والابتسامة التي تُشغَّل وتُطفأ. وتُكتب النظرة سطرًا صامتًا بعد نقطتين.
- **كتابة الصورة والمؤثرات:** صورة واحدة مكثفة مع تشبيه، بلا مصطلحات كاميرا تقريبًا. خيال إيمي تحت الماء مكتوب بترويسة عادية، وتكمله جملة صوتية بدأت في المشهد السابق.
- **٣ تقنيات نأخذها لفيلمنا القصير:**
  1. ترويسة محايدة وصادقة لظهورات "الحلم" لا تكشف طبيعتها.
  2. عدّادان زمنيان يلتقيان عند التويست، وبطاقة SUPER واحدة تعيد تفسير كل ما سبق.
  3. أشياء صغيرة مرتبطة بكل ظهور للمرأة، تتجمع عند الكشف في حقيبة الزوجة الحقيقية.

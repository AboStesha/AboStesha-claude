# Shutter Island (2010) — screenplay study

## 0. Draft facts
- **Writer:** screenplay by Laeta Kalogridis, adapted from the novel by Dennis Lehane (title page, PDF p. 1).
- **Draft:** October 3, 2007, Writer's Draft. Every page carries the header Kalogridis/SHUTTER ISLAND/10-3-07.
- **Pages:** 136 PDF pages.
  - p. 1: title page.
  - p. 2: epigraph from T.S. Eliot's *The Hollow Men*.
  - pp. 3–136: the script. The printed page number is the PDF page minus 2. All refs below are PDF pages.
- **Source:** a scanned photocopy with a garbled OCR layer. The script was read from all 136 page images, and every quote was checked against the image.
- **Scene count:** about 90 sluglines, plus about 9 unslugged flash or flashback inserts.
- **Numbering:** scenes are not numbered. There are no margin numbers, no letter suffixes and no OMITTED. This is a writer's draft.

## 1. Format & page organization
- **Sluglines** follow `INT./EXT. PLACE -- TIME` and are unnumbered.
  - Times of day are fine-grained (DUSK, TWILIGHT, DAWN) to track the two-day storm clock.
  - Modifiers include `-- CONTINUOUS` (p. 12) and `-- MINUTES LATER` (p. 46). One slugline even reads `EXT. UNDERWATER` (p. 115).
  - Caps-only lines inside action work as shots. The lighthouse first appears this way (p. 25).
- **CONTINUED / (MORE) / (CONT'D):** there are no CONTINUED headers. `(MORE)` marks speeches broken across a page (pp. 11, 87, 121). `(CONT'D)` appears whenever a speaker resumes.
- **Transitions (about 37):**
  - `CUT TO:` (about 23) is the default.
  - `DISSOLVE TO:` (5) and `TIME CUT TO:` (2) mark time passing.
  - `SMASH CUT TO:` is used only to wake Teddy from dreams (pp. 42, 77, 78).
  - The writer coins `CUT TO MORE DREAM:` to chain dreams (pp. 75, 77).
  - `FLASH TO WHITE` brackets the one true flashback (pp. 129, 133).
- **Other conventions:**
  - SUPERIMPOSE gives the date (p. 3) and TWO WEEKS LATER (p. 135).
  - An SFX PRELAP of retching opens the script (p. 3).
  - Camera terms (POV, WIDEN, PAN UP) are rare and saved for reveals.
  - O.S. (about 10) covers unseen voices. V.O. (about 7) narrates over flashes.
- **Flash grammar:** each flash opens with an underlined header (QUICK FLASHES OF IMAGES, p. 18), is set in italics, and closes with `BACK TO SCENE`. The Dachau memory is INTERCUT under `TEDDY (V.O.)` (pp. 59–61).
- **Labeling the reality layers:**
  - **Memories:** italic flash blocks plus a stated color code. The wife is in bright color (p. 6); Dachau is in black and white (p. 18).
  - **Dreams:** a slugline suffix marks them, and they end with a waking action (pp. 42, 78):
    > `INT. APARTMENT -- DAY -- TEDDY'S DREAM` (p. 39)
  - **One false awakening is deliberately unlabeled** (p. 78). It is the dream that plants the wet-wife line.
  - **Waking hallucinations:** never in the slugline. They are flagged in the action (p. 89), and once by a writer's note:
    > `(Note: Dolores is Teddy's hallucination, so Cawley doesn't see or respond to her.)` (p. 118)
  - **The cave woman (pp. 98–107):** never flagged at all.
  - **The truth:** the flashback gets a `-- FLASHBACK` suffix and a desaturated look written on the page (p. 129).
- **Unusual devices:**
  - Writer's notes to the reader (pp. 18, 59, 118).
  - A narrator who doubts that the "found" Rachel is the same woman (p. 70).
  - **Cue engineering:** the cave woman is cued `RACHEL 2`. After the reveal the cues become `TEDDY/ANDREW` and `DR. LESTER SHEEHAN`, so the identity shift is carried by the cue itself.

## 3. Twist engineering
**The twist:** Marshal Teddy Daniels is the missing 67th patient, Andrew Laeddis. He shot his wife after she drowned their children. The case, the partner and the escapee are a role-play staged by his doctors.

**Second twist:** he relapses, or pretends to, so that he can choose the lobotomy.

**Missing-partner backstory:**
- *Over-specified:* Chuck's cover is detailed (Seattle office, Japanese girlfriend, war scar; pp. 5–6, 52), which makes him feel real.
- *Slightly off:* each detail doesn't quite fit. Teddy first places him in the wrong city, and the action notes Chuck revealing more than he meant to (pp. 5–6).
- *Vanishing like Rachel:* when Chuck later disappears exactly as Rachel did, we read it as more conspiracy rather than as proof he never existed.

**Misdirection toolkit:**
- *Strict POV:* every scene includes Teddy, so staff knowledge leaks out only as behavior.
- *Genre cover:* 1950s paranoia (HUAC, Nazi doctors, psychosurgery) gives every oddity a conspiracy reading.
- *Displacement:* his own crime is told to him as someone else's case.
- *Dual-explanation clues:* drugs or withdrawal; coaching for a cover-up or for a play.
- *Truth from unreliable voices:* the inmate, Noyce, the cave woman.
- *Red herrings:* the scarred Laeddis, the Warden.
- *Alibis:* the roof leak, the storm.
- *Withholding:* "Edward" and "Chanal" are never spoken before p. 120.
- *Editing grammar:* the flashes are true, but of the wrong trauma.

**The reveal (pp. 116–135, about 20 pp, ~15% of the script):**
- *The set:* it is a prepared set: a waiting towel, a running tape recorder and sheet-covered easels (p. 117).
- *The opening line:* Cawley's first line is Teddy's own dream line:
  > "Why're you all wet, baby?" (p. 116)
- *The sequence:*
  - Objects are unveiled like a lecture: "patient" (p. 119), the anagram easel (p. 120), Chuck entering as Sheehan (p. 124), the water pistol (p. 127), the crime photos (p. 128).
  - The dead daughter's touch then opens a 4.5-page desaturated flashback.
  - A question-and-answer confession closes the sequence (pp. 133–135).
- *What gets re-read:* Chuck, the staff, the dreams, the symptoms, and Cawley's "case":

> "To sustain that structure, she employs an elaborate narrative thread to her life that is completely fictitious. And she gives us all parts to play in that fiction." (p. 19)

**Fair play: mostly fair.**
- *What's on the page:* by the 65% mark it has shown the lake, the three children, the wet wife, a partner he never met, the 67th patient and the "stage play".
- *The anagram can't be solved ahead,* because "Edward" and "Chanal" are withheld; it only decodes after the fact.
- *The cave woman is presented as objective.* She carries the main red herring and is debunked in one line (p. 123).

## 4. Dialogue craft
- **Voice differentiation:**
  - *Teddy:* clipped, profane, procedural; he closes exchanges with a jab (p. 11).
  - *Chuck:* wisecracks, and his tag "boss", which is also the last word he speaks (p. 136).
  - *Cawley:* long clinical sentences; he corrects "prisoner" to "patient" (pp. 17, 23).
  - *Naehring:* psychoanalytic labels (pp. 33–35).
  - *The Warden:* sermon cadence (pp. 107–109).
  - *Patients:* each gets one fixation (cold feet p. 45; the H-bomb p. 83).
  - *Dolores:* lyrical and sensory, against the institution's voice (pp. 41, 89).
- **Subtext:**
  1. *Smoke, not fire (p. 6).* On the surface he is saying she didn't suffer. Underneath, he needs a clean, assignable death, and the fire anchors the lie.
  2. *Getting over it is everyone's job (p. 20).* This is the exact rationalization that let him ignore Dolores (p. 128).
  3. *Luck doesn't work that way (p. 64).* It sounds like conspiracy talk; it is really the therapist nudging him to doubt his story.
- **Exposition hiding:**
  - Interrogation scenes (pp. 26–29, 44–51).
  - Turf fights over guns and files.
  - Displacement into a case file and into dreams.
  - A confession forced when Chuck angrily demands the truth (pp. 52–55).
  - Objects (the note, the intake form, the easels).
  - A joke at each info spike (p. 28).
- **Rhythm:**
  - Speeches mostly run 1–3 lines, with constant interruptions.
  - Long speeches are rationed to set pieces (Dachau, pp. 59–61; the cave; the reveal).
  - Silence is written as action (pp. 12, 104).
- **Lines that flip after the twist:**
  - The self-pep-talk (p. 3).
  - Meeting the "new partner" (p. 5).
  - Fleeing what one has done (p. 36).
  - "We just met" (p. 52).
  - Denying he is a patient (p. 105).
- **Excerpts:**
  - > "Four people died. It was the smoke that got her. Not the fire. That's important." (p. 6)

    Over-insistence flags the fabrication.
  - > "Everything you were up to. Your whole plan. This is a game. A stage play." (p. 88)

    The literal truth, hidden inside a rant we read as conspiracy.
  - > "He's not calling me Laeddis. You switched the emphasis -- he was saying this was about me, and Laeddis." (p. 122)

    A comma is the twist: Noyce's line on p. 87 reads both ways.
  - > "Seems to me... it's better to die a good man, than live as a monster." (p. 136)

    It answers Cawley's diagnosis (p. 121). The tag "Don't you think?" makes it a choice put to Sheehan.

## 5. Directing actors on the page
- **Parentheticals:** about two per page, roughly one speech in four. Types:
  - delivery: `(wry)`, `(deadpan)`;
  - playing against the line: `(feigns innocent surprise)` (p. 33), and `(pleasantly)` placed just before an insult (p. 16);
  - micro-hesitation: `(tiny hesitation)` (p. 92);
  - realization: `(realizing)` (p. 73);
  - break points: `(almost a sob)` (p. 41), `(voice breaking)` (p. 134).
- **Behavior carries the emotion:**
  - a hangnail picked with eyes down (p. 27);
  - eyes that well up while the face stays frozen (p. 51);
  - Breene recoiling as if from a rattlesnake (p. 49);
  - a calm face turning predatory as a pencil scratches (pp. 46–48);
  - a shaking hand shoved under a leg (p. 105).
- **The key device is the performance inside the performance.** The staff are actors in Cawley's play, so the page gives them exactly what an amateur actor leaks:
  - a split-second hesitation (pp. 22, 50);
  - a stifled laugh (p. 28);
  - a glance to the "director", who answers with an imperceptible head shake (p. 71);
  - a voice that almost breaks on "boss" (p. 136).

  Teddy, meanwhile, is playing the game for his life (p. 111).

## 6. Writing images, VFX & sound
- **Description style:**
  - Present tense, caps on the key noun and verb, paragraphs of 1–3 lines.
  - Similes do the lighting (a hawk-on-a-mouse stare, p. 74).
  - Each impossible image gets one line and one hard verb: a bottle dissolving into smoke (p. 39), a window turning from city to lake (p. 40), water pouring from a charred woman (p. 41), Laeddis becoming Chuck (p. 76), a drowned girl's soundless mouth (p. 77).
- **Implied camera:** the migraine POV is written as cobwebs pierced by spikes of light (p. 73). The dream bleeds into the waking scene:
  > "Teddy STARTS AWAKE, GASPING for breath. He looks down at his hands -- which are SOAKING WET." (p. 42)
- **The color grade is a narrative code, stated plainly:**
  > "The Dolores flashbacks have been BRIGHTLY COLORED, the Dachau scenes BLACK AND WHITE -- but this is somewhere IN BETWEEN." (p. 129)
- **The reveal object cashes in the motif:**
  > "WATER arcs from the pistol, hits a startled Cawley in the CHEST, then the FACE. His gun is a water pistol." (p. 127)
- **Sound on the page:**
  - a retching prelap (p. 3);
  - tinny Mahler bridging the mansion and Dachau (pp. 31–35);
  - rain written as tears and sobs (p. 53);
  - a dying match that ends a hallucination (p. 90);
  - the killing shot as a thunderclap in silence (p. 132).
- **Transitions as storytelling:**
  - One lake image is chained four times: the dream window (p. 40), the dream clearing (p. 77), the crime photo (p. 128), and the flashback, which names it the same view (p. 130).
  - SMASH CUT always means waking up; FLASH TO WHITE always means the truth.

## 7. Theme, motifs, meaning
- **Central dramatic question.**
  - *Surface:* will the marshal find Rachel and Laeddis and expose Ashecliffe?
  - *Deep:* will Andrew accept what he did, and can he live with it?
- **Theme:** guilt builds merciful fictions, and the cure is a truth that may be unbearable.
  - It is hidden early as a line about other men (p. 36).
  - It is stated as clinical theory (p. 19) and as method (talk therapy, p. 43; an honest reckoning, p. 126).
  - It is enacted as a choice at the end (p. 136).
  - The Eliot epigraph names the gap between the idea and the reality (p. 2).
- **Motifs:**
  - **Water = truth.** The phobia (p. 3), the dream leak (p. 42), the wet-wife line three times (pp. 78, 116, 130), the water pistol (p. 127), blood like dream water (p. 133).
  - **Fire = the lie.** The fire story (p. 6), the charred wife (p. 40), the "firebug" (p. 54), Teddy's own arson (p. 114). Finally the fire is returned to Dolores, who set it (p. 128).
  - **The floral tie.** Her gift (p. 6); burned as a fuse on his way to the truth (p. 113).
  - **Hands.** They shake throughout (pp. 79, 105, 118) and are steady only at the killing (p. 132).
  - **The girl.** A Dachau corpse (p. 18), then a dream accuser (p. 75), then his daughter Rachel (p. 128).
- **Final image.** He walks toward orderlies who are unrolling glinting steel. PAN UP to the lighthouse, written as staring blindly: the eye where truth was found and where the self will be cut away (p. 136).
- **The final line is in this draft**, as a statement plus a tag question. Its ambiguity lives in the character cue:
  - He stays `TEDDY/ANDREW`, never plain Andrew.
  - The doctors read "Chuck" as a relapse; only Sheehan and the audience hear a lucid choice.
  - The released film turns the line into an open question, which makes it more ambiguous.

## 8. Lessons for a 7-minute twist/VFX short
**Parallel:**
- Ashecliffe = our lab.
- Cawley's role-play = the wife's "dream" protocol.
- Chuck = the woman, the companion who is secretly the caregiver.
- Teddy = our man, who thinks he is the investigator/creator but is the patient.

The shared thesis:

> "I swore before the entire board of overseers that I could construct the most extravagant role-play in the history of psychiatry, and it would save you. Bring you back." (p. 125)

1. **The caregiver co-stars in the fiction** — the partner Teddy "just met" is his psychiatrist (pp. 5, 124) — give the woman a first line that is true under both readings.
2. **The authority describes the hero as someone else's case** — Cawley's "Rachel" speech (p. 19) — the lab log explains how *she* is assembled from memory; it is really about *him*.
3. **One tell per performer-within-the-performance** — the fumbled snap (p. 12), the blink (p. 50), the glance to the director (p. 71) — she reaches for an object before he has "created" it. Two or three tells at most.
4. **Symptoms with two explanations** — the tremors look like drugs (p. 104) and turn out to be withdrawal (p. 119) — his headaches look like side-effects and turn out to be memory returning.
5. **Color-code the layers once, in writing** — pp. 6, 18, 129, plus the slug suffix (p. 39) — the lab cool, his "construct" warm, the true memory desaturated; a `-- HIS CONSTRUCT` suffix.
6. **One motif crosses the layers and becomes the reveal object** — water: dream, then soaked hands (p. 42), then the water pistol (p. 127) — a ring or a song.
7. **One line, three speakers** — the wet-wife line is said by the dream wife, then the doctor, then the hero (pp. 78, 116, 130) — she speaks his old proposal line, and later we learn it was his.
8. **A prepared reveal set, on a clock** — the towel, the running recorder and the covered easels (p. 117); a two-day limit (p. 125); an earlier breakthrough he lost (p. 134) — one shot of a session counter at #37, "last session allowed", gives the loop and the stakes in seconds.
9. **Truth early, in a voice we discount** — Noyce (p. 88) — the lab AI says something true and dismissible in minute one.
10. **End on a choice the audience witnesses** — the final line and its tag (p. 136) — he chooses to remember her, at a cost. Invert the tone: Shutter's staff leak fear; ours leak tenderness.

## 9. البطاقة العربية
- **الفيلم:** Shutter Island (2010) · **الكاتب:** لاييتا كالوغريديس، عن رواية دينيس ليهان (مسودة الكاتب، ٣ أكتوبر ٢٠٠٧) · **عدد الصفحات:** ١٣٦ صفحة PDF، منها ١٣٤ صفحة نص. النسخة ممسوحة ضوئياً.
- **التويست في جملة:** المارشال تيدي دانيلز هو نفسه المريض رقم ٦٧ «أندرو ليديس»، الذي قتل زوجته بعد أن أغرقت أطفالهما الثلاثة. التحقيق كله، بما فيه الشريك والمريضة الهاربة، مسرحية علاجية أعدّها أطباؤه ليواجه الحقيقة.
- **آلية التويست:**
  1. **الإزاحة:** يروي الطبيب جريمة البطل كأنها حالة مريضة أخرى (ص ١٧–١٩)، فيسمع البطل قصته ولا يتعرّف عليها.
  2. **أدلة بتفسيرين:**
     - الصداع والارتجاف يبدوان تسميماً بالأدوية (ص ١٠٤)، ثم يتبيّن أنهما أعراض انسحاب (ص ١١٩).
     - عداء الحرّاس ليس مؤامرة، بل خوف من مريض خطير.
  3. **موتيف يعبر طبقات الواقع:** الماء في الحلم، ثم اليدان المبتلّتان عند الاستيقاظ (ص ٤٢)، ثم مسدس ماء في لحظة الكشف (ص ١٢٧).
- **سرّ الحوار:**
  - الحقيقة تُقال مبكراً على لسان شخص نعدّه مجنوناً، فلا نصدّقها.
  - جملة واحدة تُقرأ قراءتين بمجرد نقل النبر أو الفاصلة (ص ١٢٢).
  - اقتباس: "This is a game. A stage play." (p. 88)
- **إدارة الممثل على الورق:** الطاقم كله «ممثلون داخل المسرحية»، فيمنحهم النص علامات الممثل المرتبك:
  - تردّد لحظي.
  - نظرة خاطفة إلى «المخرج» (ص ٧١).
  - صوت يكاد ينكسر (ص ١٣٦).
- **كتابة الصورة والمؤثرات:**
  - كود لوني معلن صراحة على الصفحة: ذكريات الزوجة بألوان زاهية، وداخاو بالأبيض والأسود، والحقيقة بألوان باهتة (ص ١٢٩).
  - كل صورة مستحيلة تُكتب في سطر واحد بفعل قوي.
  - ننتقل بين الطبقات بقطع حادّ (SMASH CUT) عند الاستيقاظ، وبوميض أبيض (FLASH TO WHITE) عند الوصول إلى الحقيقة.
- **٣ تقنيات نأخذها لفيلمنا القصير:**
  1. اجعل المرأة «المحلوم بها» هي المعالِجة الحقيقية، وامنحها جملة أولى صادقة في القراءتين.
  2. اختر موتيفاً واحداً، خاتماً أو أغنية، يتسرّب من الحلم إلى المختبر ثم يصبح أداة الكشف.
  3. صوّر الكشف كموقع مُعَدّ سلفاً، مع عدّاد جلسات يُظهر أنها ليست المحاولة الأولى، ثم اختم باختيار واعٍ يشهده الجمهور وحده.

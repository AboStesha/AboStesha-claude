# Mr. Nobody (2009) — screenplay study

All page refs are PDF pages (p.N). Percent = p ÷ 125. Script page numbers printed in the footer = PDF page − 1.

## 0. Draft facts
- **Writer as credited:** `Written by` / `Jaco Van Dormael` (title page, p.1). Sole credit.
- **Draft label / date as printed:** `Version 11` (title page, bottom left), and the header `"Mr. NOBODY" VERSION 11` on every script page. No date is printed. *PDF metadata only, not printed:* Final Draft file `Mr NOBODY VA 11HPM.fdr Scénario`, created 1 Oct 2007. The English carries traces of a French original: `(OFF)` for every voice, `bis` inserts, the AZERTY keyboard row (p.91), typos like `SUPERIMPOSTION` (p.123).
- **Pages:** 125 PDF pages: an unnumbered title page plus script pages 1–124.
- **Scene count:** 305 numbered scenes (1–305, no gaps, no OMITTED), plus 164 lettered sub-scenes in 76 scenes and two inline lettered flash lists (31 items in sc.285, 10 in sc.295).
- **Numbered?** Yes, left side only, inside the bold heading line. Differences from the released film are not assessed.

## 1. Format & page organization
The full replication spec is in 1b. At a glance:
- **Scene numbers:** left only, bold, `N - SLUG`; bold sub-labels `NA -` open action paragraphs.
- **Sluglines:** INT./EXT. LOCATION — TIME. A hyphen sometimes replaces the em dash (p.3, 26, 34). Twenty headings swap INT/EXT for a mode word (FLASH, MODEL, VISION…).
- **CONTINUED / (MORE) / (CONT'D):** no CONTINUED. Split speeches get `(MORE)` and the software's `(cont'd)` (p.23–24, 68–69, 119–120). The writer types `(CONT.)` when a speaker resumes after action (112×).
- **Transitions:** none (no CUT TO, DISSOLVE, FADE, MATCH CUT or INTERCUT). Cuts are prose bridges (1b-f).
- **INSERT / SUPER / MONTAGE / FLASHBACK:** no formal labels. FLASH is a heading or an inline word (21 lines). Supers are in prose (p.102, 123), montages are lettered sub-scenes, and intercutting is alternating numbers (p.26).
- **V.O. / O.S.:** never used. `(OFF)` covers both (108×), plus voice-only speakers (TELEPHONE VOICE, COMMENTARY). Narration and broadcast dialogue are *italic* (88 of 117 OFF/VOICE blocks; the habit fades after ~p.100).
- **Times, dreams, realities:** year tags in slugs, possessive place names per life, LIMBO/UNDETERMINED/MODEL/VISION headings, replay and reverse flags in action (1b-d).

Format specimens (labels only): `1 - INT. LABORATORY — DAY` (p.2) · `2A - A morgue.` (p.2) · `34 - EXT. CHILD NEMO'S STREET (1977) — DAY` (p.19).

### 1b. Format blueprint
**a) Page & type (measured from the PDF).** The script pages are A4 (595×842 pt). The title page alone is US Letter, set in Courier Prime, which suggests it was swapped in later. The body is Courier New 12 pt, single-spaced, about 56 lines per page. Distances from the left page edge:
- action at 0.83 in, with lines about 65 characters wide;
- dialogue at 2.2 in, 3.5 in wide (about 35 characters);
- parenthetical at 2.5 in;
- character cue at 3.2 in;
- revision asterisks in a column at 7.5 in.

Action and dialogue are **fully justified**, a French typing habit. Each scene heading has two blank lines before it and one after. **Bold** is used for the heading line (number plus slug), every sub-label and the letters of inline lists, and nothing else. *Italic* is used only for narration, broadcast and read-aloud dialogue; action text is never italic. *For an English draft:* keep these relative indents but switch to US Letter, a 1.5 in left margin and ragged-right text.

**b) Header, footer, revisions.**
- Header on every script page, top right, about 0.3 in from the top: `"Mr. NOBODY" VERSION 11`.
- Footer: a centred page number with spaced hyphens, `- 1 -`. The title page has no number.
- Revisions: one `*` in the right margin on each changed line, 463 in total, appearing on 105 of the 124 script pages. There are no coloured pages, no revision dates, no A-pages and no locked-page marks. The version number is the only revision identity.
- Page breaks never get CONTINUED. A split speech ends with `(MORE)` and resumes at the top of the next page with the cue repeated, e.g. `ADULT NEMO (OFF)(CONT.) (cont'd)` (p.24).

**c) Scene-numbering scheme.**
- *Position and style:* the number sits on the left, on the same bold line as the slug, separated by space-hyphen-space: `43 - INT. TELEVISION STUDIO — DAY`. Numbers are consecutive integers from 1 to 305 with no gaps.
- *Letter suffixes:* a bold `2A -` opens an action paragraph and no slug is repeated. The writer uses them for five things:
  - strings of *different places* under one umbrella heading: 2A–2G are the morgue, train tracks, river, bath, void and spaceship (p.2–3);
  - time cuts in the same place (104A/104B, p.46);
  - TV and insert images (15A–C, 58A–G);
  - montage beats (27A–I, p.14–15; nine is the largest set);
  - one-line rhyme montages (217A–E, p.91).
  
  Only one sub-scene carries a slug of its own: `110A - EXT/INT. TERRACE / MOTHERS APARTMENT - MORNING` (p.48).
- *Deeper levels and inserts:* `15B1` (p.10), `241B1` and `241A2` (p.103), and the French insert suffix `64bis` / `73BIS` / `274Abis` (p.30, 32, 113).
- *Hypnotic flow:* labels prefixed with an ellipsis, `...21A - a child's feet running down a station platform`, and a return to an earlier shot written `21C(cont.)` (p.12).
- *Returning from a flash:* either the slug ends in `— DAY (CONT.)` (sc.85, p.35) or a `(cont.)` line sits under the heading (sc.37, p.21).
- *Lettered lists inside one paragraph:* bold letters running A to Z and then A1 to E1 (sc.285, p.121), or A to J (sc.295, p.124).
- *Irregularities not to copy:* 2E is skipped (p.3); 219 is printed in the order A, B, D, C (p.92); 15c is lowercase; three headings lack the space before the hyphen (`42-`, `184-`, `188A-`).

**d) Labelling lives, ages, timelines, realities.**
- *The slug's place name is the main index of which life we are in.*
  - Mother branch: MOTHER'S APARTMENT, NEMO'S ROOM IN HIS MOTHER'S HOUSE, ANNA AND NEMO'S HOUSE, NEMO'S INDUSTRIAL LOFT.
  - Father branch: FATHER'S HOUSE / YARD, NEMO AND ELISE'S HOUSE, NEMO AND JEAN'S YARD, JEAN AND NEMO'S ROOM.
  - Frame: HOSPITAL ROOM, DOCTOR'S OFFICE.
  - Fiction inside the fiction: SPACESHIP, BUS ON MARS.
- *Year tags* go in parentheses before the time of day, and only at entry points and anchors (16 headings): `(1977)` p.19, `(1988)` p.45 and 57, `(2006)` p.30 and 70, `(2092)` p.77 and 119. One tag spans a jump inside a single scene: `81 - INT. NEMO'S ROOM IN HIS MOTHER'S HOUSE (1980/1984) — DAY` (p.34), where the camera circles the room and comes back to a 14-year-old.
- *Cues do not carry the life.* Plain `NEMO` appears 175 times, in every branch. ANNA, ELISE and JEAN keep the same cue at every age (`TEENAGE ELISE` appears once, p.53). Age prefixes mark a *voice register* rather than a life:
  - `OLD NEMO` is the 2092 frame (about 45 cues);
  - `CHILD NEMO (OFF)` is the narrator (27);
  - `ADULT NEMO` is mostly the TV lecturer;
  - `TEENAGE NEMO (OFF)` reads his Mars novel aloud.
- *Ages are given in action lines.* When a character reaches a new age, the name is capitalised again as if introducing someone new: CHILD NEMO is SEVEN (p.9), FOURTEEN YEARS OLD (p.34), ADULT ANNA (p.39), TEENAGE JEAN (p.59). Physical tags then stick to each life: a burn scar (p.47, 68, 102); hair that is short (p.77), long (p.76, 84) or in a ponytail (p.107); `Nemo — the widow -` (p.108).
- *Mode and unreal-space headings:*
  - `36 - FLASH`, `252 - FLASH — DREAM`, `51 - VISION`, `32 - MODEL`;
  - `25 - INT. LIMBO — DAY`, `39 - INT. UNDETERMINED — DAY`;
  - `173 - INT. OR EXT. NEMO AND ELISE'S HOUSE OR YARD — DAY`, `236 - INT. THEATER/BEAUTY PARLOR — DAY`, `198 - EXT. SKY THEN CITY — DAY`;
  - multi-place strings use `INT./EXT. MISC. — DAY/NIGHT`.
- *Reality slips are written into the cue:* `MOTHER (VOICE OF CHILD NEMO)` (p.18), `JEAN (ELISE'S VOICE)` (p.90), and the parenthetical `(with the voice of child Nemo)` (p.120).
- *Replays and rewinds are flagged at the top of the action:* `The scene is identical.` (p.6), `...Repetition of the same scene.` (p.31), `Back in time...` (p.58, 69), `shot in reverse` (p.21, 37, 82).
- *Superposition scene:* three branches are compressed into one number (sc.47, three weddings with three brides, p.25).
- *No chapter or title cards.* On-screen titles are written as prose: the credits (p.2, 5), `ENTROPY` (p.102), `LAST WORDS` (p.123).

**e) Many very short scenes, hard jumps.**
- There are 2.46 numbered scenes per page, or about 3.8 labelled units per page once sub-labels are counted. The median scene is 8 non-blank lines; 29% of scenes are 4 lines or fewer and 11% are 2 lines or fewer. Even these get a full numbered heading, e.g. `249 - FLASH UNDER WATER` with a single sentence (p.105).
- Intercutting is done by alternating numbers (street / chicken barn / street / cookie factory / street, p.26).
- Sentences run across cuts: 49 scenes end on `...` and 10 begin with `...`. In three places a colon hands off to the next heading, e.g. a line ending `In the rearview mirror we see:` (p.56) leads into `...reflected in the motorcycle rearview mirror` (p.57). The body waking is used as the cut itself (p.2–3, 5, 6, 13, 111).
- Time is compressed inside a scene with `A bit later.` paragraphs (p.45–46), chains of `Then…` (p.85) and paragraphs opening with `(...)` (p.88–89).

**f) Transitions.** All of them are camera paths or rhymes written into action:
- into a reflection in an eye, out to a train window (p.38);
- into a photograph (p.39);
- out of a window until the city freezes into *a postcard on the other life's kitchen table*, crossing branches (p.44–45);
- a swing compared to the pendulum (p.13);
- steam drawn through a duct up to the clouds (p.87);
- a stage curtain rising and falling (p.100–101).

Sound also cuts: alarm clocks (p.3, 5, 6), Indian film music carried from one scene to the next (p.68), typewriter keys leading into the spaceship (p.91).

## 3. Twist engineering
**The twist in one sentence:** every life we have watched is imagined by a nine-year-old on a station platform in the instant before he must choose between his mother and father. That includes the 120-year-old in 2092 who seems to be remembering them.

**Misdirection toolkit**
- **Genre expectation (the frame):** a satirical 2092 frame and an interview make the oldest self the teller. The reveal hands authorship to the youngest self.
- **Unreliability declared up front:** confused memory is diagnosed (p.10), and Old Nemo calls himself a man who mixes everything up (p.20). Contradictions read as symptoms, not clues.
- **Dream grammar:** alarm-clock wake-ups (p.3, 5, 6, 13) file every impossibility as dream noise.
- **Rules disguised as a job:** superstition, chaos, string theory and entropy arrive as Nemo's (or Peter's) TV show, so the theory reads as characterisation.
- **Emotional cover:** Elise's breakdown (p.70–99) sits over the artificiality clues (price tag p.78, scale-model street p.90).
- **Red herrings and withholding:** the code, the suicide-note question (p.62), Daniel Jones (p.96–97). The architect is named as an abstraction (p.117) before being identified.

**The reveal: p.113–121, about 9 pages (90–97%), staged three ways.**
1. **Object + recorded message (p.116–118).** On a video recorder in Anna's abandoned house, Old Nemo reads Nemo's replies from a typed transcript and warns him about a chair just before it breaks. Flashes show how Nemo was never born: his parents miss each other, a sledding death, a dying sperm, a prehistoric woman.
2. **Sound device (p.119–120).** Old Nemo says, "We only live in the imagination of a nine-year-old child." The child's voice takes over, synced to the old man's lips, and the journalist turns to find himself at a train window.
3. **Lettered recap (p.120–121).** Strings A–M (toward the mother) and N–E1 (toward the father), each flash containing the others; then the sea is dismantled (p.121).

The audience re-reads the dream oddities, price tags, models, shared lines and voice swaps; the prologue deaths become endings; and the name changes meaning. Nemo is Latin for "nobody", and the reporter says he officially doesn't exist (p.11).

**Fair-play verdict.** Scrupulously fair, almost over-planted. The script never hides *what* we see, only *who is dreaming*. The weak spots are identifiers that drift:
- the child is 7 (p.9, 13, 100), 8 (p.24, 34) and 9 (p.43, 120);
- the 05:50 deadline (p.118) does not match the death at 5:17 (p.123);
- the colours of the girls' dresses swap (p.24 vs 53).

## 4. Dialogue craft
- **Voice differentiation:**
  - CHILD NEMO (OFF): fairy-tale tautologies and chains of why (p.15–18).
  - OLD NEMO: crank, profane, aphoristic, always asking the time (p.19–23, 114).
  - The presenter: cascades of rhetorical questions, numbers, Latin names (p.23–28, 75, 102).
  - Teen Nemo: monosyllables (p.37), a numbered manifesto (p.60), facts as shields.
  - Anna: teasing frankness as a teen (p.41–42); long, frightened, measured sentences as an adult (p.85–86).
  - Elise: self-accusation and demands (p.70–72). Jean: tests of attention.
  - The journalist: literal either/or questions (p.42, 100, 119), the audience's confusion voiced.
- **Subtext:** Jean's coffee quiz (below) indicts a marriage. Nemo's dinner-table prophecy of the lover's car crash (p.35) really means: leave my mother; he wets himself afterwards. Nemo, who cannot swim (p.29, 40), answers Elise's drowning image by promising they will learn (p.99).
- **Exposition hiding:** TV formats carry it: a reality host and a vote (p.10–11), a Mars ad (p.11), lectures. Old Nemo's jokes deliver the future world (p.20). Objects carry facts: an LCD newspaper (p.4), a postcard (p.45), a `NO LONGER AT THIS ADDRESS` stamp (p.73), Jean's found note (p.62).
- **Rhythm:** speeches run 1–3 lines; long ones are reserved for lectures and Old Nemo. Silence is written as a `...` dialogue line (p.8, 41, 51, 52, 96). Overlap gets a parenthetical (p.14). There is a one-upping phone volley of love (p.55–56) and vows echoed back as rhyme (p.49).
- **Lines that change after the twist (paraphrased):** the pigeon's question repeated over the morgue (p.2); his name, Nobody (p.3); asking Jean if he is dead (p.7); needing to go back and start again (p.56, 102); the continuity girl's one-take rule (p.104); his last words, the most beautiful day (p.123).

Exemplary excerpts:
- "Gravity on Mars is .38. Three times less than on Earth." (p.52). *Why it works:* a fact used as armour. Elise hears charm, and the line plants the Mars escape life.
- "You love all that, but me, do you love me?" (p.78). *Why it works:* it answers his catalogue of her parts by naming the whole he left out. His reply is about need, not love.
- "Do I take sugar in my coffee? Can you please answer me?" (p.90). *Why it works:* a trivial question becomes a verdict on the marriage.
- "Each word you think you are inventing is here on this paper." (p.117). *Why it works:* the scene is staged as a reading, so the line is a comment on the screenplay itself.

## 5. Directing actors on the page
- **Parentheticals are rare:** 32 across 693 cues (5%). They cover:
  - delivery and volume, 9 (whispering ×4, howling…);
  - who is being addressed, 4 (to himself, to the guest);
  - small actions, 7 (looking at his watch, he sighs);
  - voice and technical notes, 8 (on the screen, reading, with the child's voice ×4);
  - only 3 emotion labels (aggravated, as if distractedly).
- **The body leaks what the character won't say:** urine (p.31, 35), blushing (p.24, 38, 41), goosebumps (p.40, 43), red eyes (p.30), a hand held over a flame (p.62, 92), polishing the car all day (p.88–89). Interior beats are stated as flat facts: he hates himself (p.38), a character registers the blow (p.76, 98).
- **Masks are scripted with verbs like pretend, act as if, force herself** (p.35, 41, 43, 60), so the actor plays both the mask and the leak. A **split point of view** shows the partner's blindness: Elise's tears are seen by the camera, not by Nemo (p.6, 70).
- **Repetition with one difference gives the actor something exact to play.** On the second morning he is less lively and drops eggshell into the omelette (p.6).
- Exemplary line: "He hesitates. He is halfway between his father and his mother. A urine stain spreads over his pants." (p.31). The whole thesis is played by a nine-year-old's body.

## 6. Writing images, VFX & sound
- **Length and syntax.** VFX paragraphs run 2–6 lines of plain present-tense statements. The impossible is written as documentary fact: helicopters assembling the sea (p.6); stagehands winching the sun into place (p.119).
- **One hero object per effect:** a leaf (p.16), a drop (p.65, 86), ink (p.86), bicycles (p.110), a typewriter (p.124).
- **Camera implied by "we".** We follow the leaf (p.16); we pull back from the galaxies until they become a tear on Anna's eyelid (p.65); we travel through the steam duct (p.87). "The camera" appears only rarely (p.34, 44). Effects are timed to words: the house grows and the car turns red at the moment the voice-over names them (p.60).
- **The effect is rehearsed inside the story.** In his garage lab, time-lapse footage plays backwards: rot reverses and a dead mouse revives (p.105–106). That prepares the universe reversing (p.124).
- Examples:
  - "A drop falls from the bathroom faucet, its fall slowing... and it stops in mid air, as if time had stopped." (p.65)
  - "Hundreds of black bicycles with chrome handlebars spin in slow motion in the void, dispersing toward infinity, toward the stars, to an accompaniment of classical music." (p.110)
- **Sound on the page.** A deafening roar (p.2); ALARM in capitals (p.3); tick-tocks (p.24); only their breathing (p.44); a heartbeat slowing (p.110). The score becomes part of the story when an orchestra rides past on a bus (p.111). The missing-e lines (p.101) are a sound glitch, typed.
- **Transitions as storytelling.** The postcard moves us from one life into the other (p.44–45). The toy car becomes the real car (p.25–26). A Mars passenger's line reappears as typed text in the Elise-life office (p.98).

## 7. Theme, motifs, meaning
**Central dramatic question.** Which life is real, and what will the boy on the platform choose? The journalist asks it outright (p.42, 119). **Stated theme**, given only to unreliable or comic voices:
- Old Nemo says every path is the right one (p.119);
- he quotes Tennessee Williams, then undercuts it with a joke (p.120);
- he says he fears not having lived enough more than he fears dying (p.114).

The real theme is carried by the structure.

**Why it reads as deep: each idea is performed, not preached.**
1. **Choice is staged as an experiment.** The basic unit of the script is a replay with *one variable changed*. That is the same method as the two experiments it opens on: the pigeon (p.2) and Lorenz rounding his weather data (p.27).
   - Station sc.67 against sc.68 (p.30–31).
   - Elise's street, where the variable is whether he lets her finish her sentence (p.58 vs 69).
   - The cyclist he passes or yields to: one minute decides who drowns (p.56 vs 104).
   - The raindrop that lands on the paper or on the shoe (p.86 vs 123).
   
   The audience runs the experiment itself.
2. **Time's arrow and entropy.** The thesis is a baby's discovery (p.24): "The smoke comes out of daddy's cigarette, but it never goes back in." The entropy lecture only asks the question (p.102–103). The answer comes in images: the A–J list (p.124) reverses each planted irreversible thing, one by one (vase, smoke, ink, mashed potato, eggshell, typed page, baby). The *shape* of the film does the same thing. It begins at the 2092 deathbed and ends at the earliest childhood moment (p.125), so Old Nemo's account of living time inverted (p.117) describes the screenplay itself.
3. **The butterfly effect is shown before it is named.** The hen → cookie factory → eggshell → forgotten handbrake chain (p.26) comes *before* the lecture (p.27–28), and the lecture then recaps those same images. The camera follows the chain itself (leaf p.16, steam p.87). The causes are never decisions: a bad shoelace (p.33), a Brazilian's boiled egg and a cheaper pair of jeans (p.87–88). Blame melts into a network, which is why the father can take the child's guilt onto himself (p.32).
4. **Agency against superstition.** The teen who vows never to leave anything to chance (p.60) becomes a man who lets a coin decide, and even the coin's spin is changed by a gust of wind (p.96). Jean's discovery is a note about a life set in cement, padded with airbags (p.62), which rhymes with the airbag that reads GAME OVER (p.110). Total control and total surrender both end the game.
5. **String theory: lives that coexist.** Six dimensions coiled together, plus perhaps a second time dimension (p.23–24), become the form of the film. We see it in crossing train tracks (p.32), three weddings in one scene (p.25), flashes that each contain the others (p.120), and a universe contained in a tear (p.65), where the intimate holds the cosmic.
6. **Love crosses lives.** Anna's name is the only signal that passes between lives: in hibernation (p.47, 67) and in his last breath (p.123). Love is lectured as pheromones (p.54–55), but it is proven by goosebumps (p.40, 43). Anna defines love as the end of keeping every option open: giving up every possible life for one (p.86). The three loves are three ways of choosing:
   - Jean, chosen by a vow, leads to numbness;
   - Elise, who loves someone else, leads to depression;
   - Anna is given and taken by chance, then restored by the child's leaf.
7. **Memory.** The frame is memory work: hypnosis and an interview. Memory runs both ways: the angels' oversight lets him remember the future (p.14, 28). Identity lives in memory, not the body: the old man insists he is nine (p.43). Love is the act of rebuilding someone from memory: Anna reconstructs his face (p.74) and smells him inch by inch (p.84).
8. **The child's choice.** The platform comes back about nine times as a flash. The resolution is not picking a parent; it is changing the frame. He is in Zugzwang (p.121), and he names the paradox: "Before, he was unable to make a choice because he didn't know what would happen. And now that he knows what will happen, he is unable to make a choice." (p.122). So he runs off sideways, perpendicular to both parents, and blows a dead leaf (p.122). His one free act is small, creative and loving. The emotional arc runs from the pigeon's complaint (p.2) to thanking the very wind and rain that once erased Anna's number (p.123).

## 8. Lessons for a 7-minute twist/VFX short
1. **Replay with one variable** — the mornings (p.5/6), the station (p.30/31), the cyclist (p.56/104) — show the "dreamed" woman's arrival twice. On the second pass she corrects one detail that only a real wife could know.
2. **Index realities in the slug, keep cues plain** (p.19, 34, 48) — make the reveal a *format* event: same scene number, new sub-letter, new truth, e.g. `7A - INT. HOSPITAL ROOM — DAY`.
3. **Hide clues inside dream grammar** — two suns and a price tag (p.5, 13, which pay off at p.78 and 111) — give the lab one "glitch", such as a hospital wristband, that the audience writes off as dream noise.
4. **One object crosses the layers** — the coin on the keychain (p.104), the envelope (p.7 → 125) — let her ring or photo exist in both the dream and the real room; its second appearance triggers the reveal.
5. **Reveal with a voice swap** — p.18, 113, 120 — her line starts as his inner V.O., then is spoken by her at his bedside. Write it into the cue: `WIFE (V.O.)` becomes `WIFE`.
6. **Make the theory part of the story, and let images prove it** — the lecture comes after the egg chain (p.26–28) — his lab log states a rule about memory (for example, that recall rebuilds a memory), and the twist turns the rule back on him.
7. **Memory is sensory** — smell and goosebumps (p.16, 40, 84). The film's own line is your premise: "Sometimes I have trouble remembering your face. So I reconstruct you in my mind, inch by inch, until I can see you again..." (p.74).
8. **Bridges, not transitions** — "..." and colon hand-offs (p.56–57) — they save lines and keep the dream fluid.
9. **Rehearse the effect in a device inside the story** — the garage time-lapse lab (p.105–106) — show the impossible rule small and early, then scale it up at the climax.
10. **Pay off the plants in a lettered list** — A–J (p.124) — a 4–6 beat lettered reveal in which each dream object turns into its real counterpart. Letters also give the VFX team a clear count of beats to bid on.

## 9. البطاقة العربية
- **الفيلم:** Mr. Nobody (2009) · **الكاتب:** جاكو فان دورمايل · **عدد الصفحات:** 125 صفحة PDF (صفحة عنوان + 124 صفحة نص)، "Version 11"، و305 مشاهد مرقّمة.
- **التويست في جملة:** كل الحيوات التي شاهدناها، بما فيها العجوز ذو المئة والعشرين عامًا في سنة 2092 الذي يبدو أنه يتذكّرها، ليست إلا خيالَ طفلٍ في التاسعة يقف على رصيف محطة قبل أن يختار بين أمه وأبيه.
- **آلية التويست:**
  1. تُزرع العلامات داخل منطق الحلم، فيمرّ المستحيل كضجيج أحلام: شمسان، وساعة من سبع ساعات، وبطاقة سعر على المصباح (ص 5–6، 13، 78).
  2. تتكرّر يد الطفل التي تحرّك النماذج المصغّرة، ويتكرّر صوت الطفل على شفاه البالغين، تمهيدًا لكشف "المهندس" (ص 17، 113، 117).
  3. يأتي الكشف على ثلاث طبقات: شريط فيديو ونصّ مكتوب سلفًا (ص 116–117)، ثم تبديل الصوت (ص 120)، ثم قائمة ومضات مرقّمة بالحروف تلخّص كل الحيوات (ص 121).
- **سرّ الحوار:**
  - لكل عمر سجلّ لغوي خاص: الطفل يحكي بلغة الحكايات، والعجوز بالسخرية والحِكَم، والمقدّم التلفزيوني بسيلٍ من الأسئلة العلمية.
  - السؤال التافه يحمل اتهامًا لزواجٍ كامل: "Do I take sugar in my coffee? Can you please answer me?" (p.90).
  - الصمت يُكتب سطرَ حوار من نقاط فقط تحت اسم الشخصية.
- **إدارة الممثل على الورق:** الأقواس نادرة (32 فقط في النص كله)، والجسد يفضح ما لا يُقال: بقعة بول تنتشر حين يتردّد الطفل بين والديه (ص 31)، وقشعريرة الجلد علامةُ الحب (ص 40).
- **كتابة الصورة والمؤثرات:** يُكتب المستحيل بجمل خبرية بسيطة كأنه وثائقي (مروحيات تجمّع البحر، ص 6)، ويرتبط كل مؤثر بشيء بطلٍ واحد: ورقة شجر، أو قطرة مطر، أو دراجات تسبح في الفضاء. والذروة قائمة مرقّمة من الانعكاسات الزمنية (ص 124).
- **٣ تقنيات نأخذها لفيلمنا القصير:**
  1. نعيد مشهد ظهور المرأة مرتين ونغيّر متغيّرًا واحدًا يفضح أن "الحلم" ذاكرةٌ حقيقية.
  2. نكتب الكشف تبديلًا للصوت ونثبّته في اسم الشخصية: صوتها الداخلي يصبح صوتها الحقيقي عند سريره.
  3. نجعل الذاكرة حسّيةً: رائحة أو لمسة تتكرّر في الحلم ثم في الواقع، كما تعيد آنا بناء وجه حبيبها في ذهنها شبرًا شبرًا (ص 74).
